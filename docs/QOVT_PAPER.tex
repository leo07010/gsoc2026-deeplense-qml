\begin{abstract}
We introduce QOVT, a Quantum Orthogonal Vision Transformer whose attention
matrices are produced by a genuine, NISQ-deployable 6-qubit gate-model
quantum circuit -- an RY+CNOT ring with $L_\mathrm{circ}{=}8$ layers and
only $T{=}384$ free angles. On strong-gravitational-lensing images from
three simulated dark-matter models, this circuit stays competitive with
the held-out AUC of a standard 8-head classical multi-head-attention
baseline ($T{=}65{,}536$) using $170\times$ fewer attention parameters,
from $N{=}500$ to the full $\sim\!25$k images per class -- it ties or
edges ahead by a fraction of a point up to $N{=}5{,}000$, but the
classical baseline pulls modestly ahead (by $0.7$--$1.4$ points) at
$N{=}8{,}000$ and at full data, so we do not claim it beats classical
attention outright. A
pre-registered, held-out-test, 10-seed evaluation ($N{=}500$/class) further
shows the circuit significantly beats an unconstrained (non-orthogonal)
baseline on one of two datasets (Model\_II: $\Delta{=}{+}15.7$pp,
Holm-corrected $p{=}0.0039$, $10/10$ seeds) but not the other
(Model\_III: $\Delta{=}{+}7.4$pp, $p{=}0.0137$, not significant) -- a
directional, dataset-dependent, unreplicated finding that we flag, not
oversell: this round used a shorter training recipe under which the
larger unconstrained baselines may have been undertrained relative to the
tiny RY+CNOT circuit, a confound we disclose and have not yet ruled out
(Section~\ref{sec:concl}). As a companion ablation we also study Givens-butterfly
decomposition, a \emph{classical} (non-quantum, no gate-model circuit at
all) construction of the same $64{\times}64$ orthogonal matrix with
$4\times$ more parameters ($T{=}1{,}536$); it fails catastrophically and
non-monotonically in about half of all runs, including at $5\times$ more
training data, showing that classical constructions of "the same" matrix
do not inherit the quantum circuit's stability. We use these results to
empirically test the Caro et al.\ generalization bound as a circuit-design
criterion and find it does not predict our observations: a circuit-depth
ablation shows accuracy \emph{increasing}, not decreasing, with more
circuit parameters at low $N$. All code, pre-registration documents, raw
results, and this manuscript's source are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Strong gravitational lensing is one of the few direct observational probes
of dark-matter substructure: the way a foreground galaxy bends light from
a background source encodes the mass distribution along the line of sight,
including sub-halos too faint or too dark to see directly
\citep{dalal2002detecting}. Different dark-matter models predict
qualitatively different substructure -- cold dark matter (CDM) produces
discrete, point-mass-like sub-halos, while ultralight axion (vortex) dark
matter produces extended, wave-like density modulations
\citep{hui2017ultralight} -- so classifying a lensing image by which
substructure model it is consistent with is, in effect, a machine-learning
test of fundamental physics. This makes the classification task
scientifically consequential in a way that goes beyond a benchmark: getting
it right, and getting it right with as few labeled (typically
simulation-generated, and expensive to produce at fidelity) images as
possible, directly bears on which dark-matter models next-generation
surveys can discriminate between.

Classical deep learning has driven essentially all recent progress on this
task. Convolutional networks were the first to show that lens-finding and
substructure classification could be automated at scale
\citep{hezaveh2017fast,lanusse2018cmu}, and CNN-based morphology
classifiers remain strong baselines for the CDM-vs-axion-vs-none problem
specifically \citep{alexander2020deep}. Simulation-based inference extends
this to full posterior estimation over sub-halo population properties
rather than a single label \citep{brehmer2019mining}. More recently, the
field has moved to attention-based architectures: Vision Transformers
\citep{dosovitskiy2021image} and, specifically for this task, a masked-autoencoder-pretrained ViT that is the current published state of the
art on DeepLense-style substructure classification \citep{prasha2025masked}.
Attention is powerful precisely because each layer learns a dense,
quadratic-in-tokens set of interactions -- but that same density means a
standard multi-head-attention layer at our model's width ($D{=}64$, 8
heads) already spends $65{,}536$ parameters on projections alone, per layer,
raising the question of whether a structurally constrained -- and
potentially quantum-native -- parameterization of attention can match this
accuracy far more cheaply.

Quantum machine learning offers exactly such a structural constraint:
unitary (here, real-orthogonal) circuits are parameter-efficient by
construction, since a circuit with $T$ free gate angles can only reach a
$T$-dimensional sub-manifold of all possible attention matrices. This is
formalized by the generalization bound of Caro et al.~\citep{caro2022} (see
also \citep{huang2021power,liu2021rigorous}), which bounds the train/test gap
by $O(\sqrt{T/N})$ and has motivated a family of quantum-attention
transformers -- Quantum Orthogonal Transformers built from Givens-rotation
("RBS") circuits \citep{cherrat2022}, quantum attention for high-energy
physics \citep{tesi2024quantum,unlu2024hybrid,comajoan2024quantum}, and
quantum self-attention for text \citep{li2022quantum} -- alongside a
broader line of work on orthogonal and unitary neural-network layers, both
quantum \citep{kerenidis2021classical,landman2022quantum} and classically
orthogonality-regularized \citep{huang2018orthogonal,fei2022ovit}. A
parallel and largely independent line of quantum ML work uses quantum
circuits as kernel or feature-map devices \citep{havlicek2019supervised,gao2018quantum} and has been applied to other high-energy-physics
classification problems \citep{terashi2021event,ngairangbam2022anomaly}.
Running any of this on real hardware, however, means confronting the
practical limits of the current NISQ era \citep{preskill2018quantum,bharti2022nisq} and the well-documented trainability failures --
barren plateaus and over-expressibility -- that shallow, highly entangled
circuits are prone to \citep{mcclean2018barren,cerezo2021cost,sim2019expressibility}. These constraints favor circuits that are not just
parameter-efficient on paper but genuinely shallow and hardware-realizable,
a distinction this paper takes seriously: several published "quantum
orthogonal attention" constructions, including the Givens-butterfly variant
we study as a control, parameterize an orthogonal matrix using an
angle-counting scheme borrowed from circuit design, but do not correspond
to any gate sequence that runs on a quantum device.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A genuinely deployable quantum circuit.} We design and
  implement RY+CNOT, a 6-qubit, 8-layer, ring-connectivity gate-model
  circuit (96 gates total: 48 single-qubit RY + 48 two-qubit CNOT,
  $T{=}384$ free angles) that
  produces the $64{\times}64$ orthogonal attention matrices used by every
  QOVT layer, and show it stays within a point or two of an 8-head
  classical multi-head-attention baseline's held-out AUC ($T{=}65{,}536$,
  $170\times$ more parameters) across the full data-efficiency sweep --
  tied or slightly ahead up to $N{=}5{,}000$, trailing by a small,
  consistent margin at $N{=}8{,}000$ and at full data.
\item \textbf{A pre-registered statistical evaluation.} Correcting a
  methodological flaw (discovery and confirmatory samples overlapping) we
  found in an earlier campaign in this project, we pre-register hypotheses,
  a held-out test split, and a Holm-Bonferroni-corrected test battery
  \emph{before} training, then run 10 seeds $\times$ 2 datasets $\times$
  5 arms at $N{=}500$/class. RY+CNOT significantly beats an unconstrained
  baseline on one of two datasets -- a real but dataset-dependent,
  unreplicated, directional finding.
\item \textbf{An empirical test of the generalization-bound design
  rationale.} A circuit-depth ablation ($T\in\{192,384,576\}$) shows
  held-out accuracy \emph{increasing} with more circuit parameters at low
  $N$, the opposite of what the Caro bound would predict if it were the
  operative mechanism -- we still favor a shallow circuit, but for NISQ
  deployability, not for a generalization-bound advantage we do not observe.
\item \textbf{A cautionary companion control.} Givens-butterfly
  decomposition constructs the same class of orthogonal matrix
  \emph{classically} (no quantum circuit exists for it), with $4\times$
  more parameters, and fails in roughly half of all runs, non-monotonically
  in $N$ -- evidence that the RY+CNOT circuit's stability is a property of
  the circuit, not of orthogonal attention in general.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:notation}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$x$ & input lensing image, $1{\times}64{\times}64$ \\
$D{=}64$ & token embedding dimension \\
$L{=}4$ & number of QO-attention layers in the backbone \\
$N_Q{=}6$ & qubits in the RY+CNOT circuit ($2^{N_Q}{=}D$) \\
$L_\mathrm{circ}{=}8$ & RY+CNOT circuit layers (default) \\
$U,V\in O(D)$ & orthogonal matrices used as query/value projections \\
$T$ & number of free circuit / layer angles \\
$N$ & training images per class \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every attention layer of QOVT needs two $D{\times}D$ orthogonal matrices,
$U$ and $V$, in place of the usual learned $W_Q,W_K,W_V$ projections. A
circuit (or, for controls, a classical parameterization) with $T$ free
scalar parameters can only reach a $T$-dimensional sub-manifold of
$O(D)$. Caro et al.~\citep{caro2022} show that for a hypothesis class with $T$
trainable parameters trained on $N$ i.i.d.\ samples, with high probability
\[
\mathcal{R}(\hat h) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat h) \;\le\; C\sqrt{T/N} \;+\;
O\!\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\Big),
\]
i.e.\ the train/test generalization gap shrinks with fewer parameters. This
bound is the design rationale several quantum-attention papers cite for
using small-$T$ circuits \citep{huang2021power,liu2021rigorous}; we adopt
it only as a \emph{heuristic} motivating our choice of a shallow circuit
below, and we test it directly as an empirical claim in
Section~\ref{sec:depth}.

\subsection{Overall Architecture (Quantum Arm)}
\label{sec:arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}(a) shows the full quantum-arm pipeline. A
$1{\times}64{\times}64$ lensing image is patch-embedded by a single
stride-8 convolution into 64 tokens of dimension $D{=}64$; a learnable
[CLS] token is prepended and a learned positional embedding added, giving
a sequence of length 65. Each of $L{=}4$ QO-attention layers computes its
own pair of orthogonal matrices $U_\ell,V_\ell$ from an independently
parameterized instance of the RY+CNOT circuit (Section~\ref{sec:circuit}),
then applies an \emph{asymmetric} orthogonal attention,
\[
A_{ij} = \mathrm{softmax}_j\!\big(x_i \cdot U x_j / \sqrt{D}\big), \qquad
\mathrm{out}_i = \sum\nolimits_j A_{ij}\,(V x_j),
\]
i.e.\ queries are left unprojected ($W_Q{=}I$), keys use $U$, and values
use $V$ -- distinct from the symmetric $W_Q{=}W_K{=}U$ design used
elsewhere in the literature (Section~\ref{sec:concl}, Limitations). A
residual connection, LayerNorm, and a standard $D{\to}4D{\to}D$ GELU MLP
(also residual) follow, and this block repeats $L{=}4$ times. The [CLS]
token's final representation is normalized and linearly projected to the
three class logits (axion / CDM / no-substructure).

\subsection{The RY+CNOT Ring Circuit}
\label{sec:circuit}

\begin{definition}[RY+CNOT ring circuit]
On $N_Q{=}6$ qubits, one \emph{layer} applies $\mathrm{RY}(\theta_q)$ to
every qubit $q\in\{0,\dots,5\}$, then a ring of CNOTs
$(0{\to}1),(1{\to}2),\dots,(4{\to}5),(5{\to}0)$. The full circuit stacks
$L_\mathrm{circ}{=}8$ such layers with independent angles, and its
$2^{N_Q}{\times}2^{N_Q}=64{\times}64$ unitary is read out as the orthogonal
matrix $U$ (a second, independently-parameterized copy of the same circuit
family produces $V$). Total free angles per attention layer:
$T = L_\mathrm{circ}\times N_Q \times 2\text{ matrices} = 8\times 6\times 2 =
96$; across all $L{=}4$ attention layers, $T_\mathrm{total}=384$.
\end{definition}

Two properties make this circuit both a genuine quantum circuit and
practical to run at scale. First, because RY and CNOT are both real-valued
gates in the computational basis, the resulting unitary is \emph{exactly}
real -- no complex amplitude ever arises -- so its action is precisely a
real $64{\times}64$ orthogonal matrix, exactly what the attention layer
needs, with no approximation or discarded imaginary part. Second, the
circuit is shallow and low-connectivity: 96 gates total (48 single-qubit
RY, 48 two-qubit CNOT), nearest-neighbor ring connectivity only (no SWAP
overhead), well
within near-term superconducting or trapped-ion coherence budgets --
genuinely NISQ-deployable \citep{preskill2018quantum,bharti2022nisq}, unlike
the Givens-butterfly control below. Because the circuit's action is a
closed-form composition of real rotations, we implement it as pure PyTorch
tensor algebra rather than through a quantum-circuit simulator such as
PennyLane \citep{pennylane}; this is numerically identical to what a
statevector simulator (or, on the real-amplitude subspace this circuit
stays in, real hardware) would produce, and trains roughly $10\times$
faster. We confirmed empirically that gradient norms stay non-vanishing
throughout training -- no barren-plateau signature -- consistent with the
circuit's shallow depth and modest qubit count.

\subsection{Training Objective and Optimization}
\label{sec:training}

QOVT is trained end-to-end with the standard 3-class cross-entropy loss on
the [CLS] logits, $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c=1}^{3} y_c \log \hat p_c$, no
auxiliary or quantum-specific loss terms. All circuit angles are ordinary
PyTorch leaf parameters and receive gradients through the same
backpropagation graph as every other layer -- no parameter-shift rule is
needed because the RY+CNOT circuit's real-rotation composition is written
directly as differentiable tensor operations. Unless noted otherwise
(Section~\ref{sec:prereg}), all experiments use AdamW ($\mathrm{lr}{=}3\times
10^{-4}$, weight decay $0.01$, $\beta{=}(0.9,0.999)$), a plain cosine
learning-rate schedule, 50 epochs, batch size 64, images normalized to
$[0,1]$ with no augmentation. We select the checkpoint with the best
\emph{validation} AUC (not the last epoch); for the pre-registered round
(Section~\ref{sec:prereg}) the validation split is further divided into a
disjoint model-selection half and a held-out test half, with the test half
touched exactly once, after checkpoint selection.

\subsection{Baselines and Controls}
\label{sec:controls}

To isolate what the RY+CNOT circuit's quantum, orthogonal, and
parameter-count properties each contribute, we compare against three
controls that share QOVT's backbone (Section~\ref{sec:arch}) but replace
the source of $U,V$:

\textbf{Givens butterfly (classical, not quantum).} $U$ is built as a
product of $\log_2 D$ layers of $D/2$ pairwise Givens rotations in a
butterfly (FFT-like) pattern -- a standard way to parameterize an
orthogonal matrix with $O(D\log D)$ angles \citep{cherrat2022}. This
construction does \emph{not} correspond to any quantum gate sequence; we
include it purely as a classical ablation on whether the orthogonality
constraint alone, independent of any quantum circuit, explains QOVT's
behavior. $T{=}192$ angles per matrix, $1{,}536$ total.

\textbf{Sham.} $U,V$ are unconstrained $D{\times}D$ linear maps
(\texttt{nn.Linear(64,64,bias=False)}) with PyTorch's default
Kaiming-uniform initialization -- not orthogonal, not structured. $T{=}32{,}768$
total. This is the "no constraint at all" floor.

\textbf{Matched (rank-1).} $U = AB$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times r},
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times D}$, an unconstrained low-rank factorization with
$T$ set to match Givens' parameter count ($r$ chosen so $T{=}1{,}024$),
used only in the pre-registered round (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}) as a
parameter-count-matched non-orthogonal control.

\textbf{Classical MHA.} Standard 8-head multi-head attention with
independent learned $Q,K,V$ projections, $T{=}16\times 64^2=65{,}536$.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We use three DeepLense-style simulated strong-lensing datasets, each a
3-class problem (no substructure / CDM subhalos / axion vortex
substructure): Model\_I and Model\_II (Euclid-like resolution, weaker vs.\
stronger axion signal respectively) and Model\_III (HST-like resolution).
Each has $\sim$25,000--29,900 images per class; we use a fixed 9:1
train/validation split (seed 42). All reported numbers are macro-averaged
one-vs-rest AUC. Training runs on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU via SLURM.

The honest picture is mixed, not a clean win for RY+CNOT. At $N{=}500$
and $N{=}5{,}000$ it is nominally ahead of classical MHA by a small
margin ($+0.4$pp, $+0.2$pp); at $N{=}3{,}000$ the two tie exactly to three
decimals. At $N{=}8{,}000$ and at full data, classical MHA pulls
\emph{ahead} of RY+CNOT by a larger and, unlike the ties above,
consistent margin in the same direction ($+1.4$pp and $+0.7$pp
respectively) -- these are single-seed numbers and should not be
over-read, but this is a real trend, not something we can wave away as
matching. We read this table as: RY+CNOT is competitive with, not
reliably better than, classical MHA across the sweep, and appears to lose
a little ground as $N$ grows past 5,000 -- despite using $170\times$
fewer attention parameters throughout. Givens, in contrast,
collapses to near-chance ($0.526$) at $N{=}500$, recovers through the
middle of the sweep, and then collapses \emph{again} at $N{=}8{,}000$
($0.558$) before working at full data. If this were memorization -- too
few parameters relative to data, or the reverse -- we would expect a
monotonic trend with $N$; instead we see the same instability recur at a
much larger training-set size, which points to an optimization-landscape
pathology specific to the Givens-butterfly parameterization rather than a
data-efficiency effect (further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

If the Caro bound were the mechanism behind RY+CNOT's low-data performance,

help, not hurt, at low $N$. We still keep the shallow 8-layer circuit as
our default -- it is what makes the circuit NISQ-deployable -- but we no
longer offer the generalization bound as the explanation for why it works.

\subsection{Pre-Registered Multi-Seed Confirmation}
\label{sec:prereg}

The single-seed results above are suggestive but not statistically
conclusive, and an earlier campaign in this project was retracted after an
audit found its "significant" claim rested on a broken sham control and a
confirmatory sample that overlapped the discovery sample. To avoid
repeating that mistake, we pre-registered this round's hypotheses, primary
tests, and Holm-Bonferroni correction procedure in a committed document
\emph{before} running any of the training jobs, and disclose every
deviation from the main protocol (Section~\ref{sec:training}) here rather
than silently: (1) a held-out test split, distinct from the
model-selection split, touched once; (2) the addition of the matched
rank-1 control; (3) a single uniform learning rate for every arm (no
quantum-specific boost); and (4) a different optimization recipe --
AdamW, $\mathrm{lr}{=}10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$,
\texttt{CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts}, 30 epochs -- used only for this round.
We run 5 arms $\times$ 2 datasets (Model\_II, Model\_III) $\times$ 10 seeds
(42--51) at $N{=}500$/class, 100 runs total.

of the two effects in this round.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\textbf{Parameter efficiency comes with spectral stability.} RY+CNOT stays
within a point or two of classical MHA's accuracy at every $N$ we test,
and is never worse than that by more than $1.4$ points, using $170\times$
fewer attention parameters (Table~\ref{tab:params}) -- because orthogonality is enforced by
construction: $\|U\|_2=\|V\|_2=1$ for every setting of the circuit's
angles, so the attention layer's output can never blow up or collapse
regardless of $T$. This is also why RY+CNOT never exhibits Givens'
catastrophic failures at any $N$ we test (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) -- both
circuits are orthogonality-constrained, so orthogonality alone does not
explain the difference; the RY+CNOT gate sequence itself is the more
stable optimization landscape.

\textbf{The generalization bound is not the operative mechanism.} If the
Caro bound explained RY+CNOT's data efficiency, we would expect it to beat
classical MHA at low $N$ (where the $O(\sqrt{T/N})$ gap should matter most)
and the depth ablation to show \emph{fewer} parameters generalizing better.
Neither holds: RY+CNOT never clearly beats classical MHA at any $N$ we
test, and actually trails it by a modest but consistent margin at the two
\emph{largest} $N$ (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) -- the opposite of where a
parameter-count-driven generalization advantage should be largest. More
circuit parameters also improve, not hurt, low-$N$ accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:depth}). We keep the bound as a design
heuristic that motivated trying a shallow circuit in the first place, not
as an explanation for the results we observe.

\textbf{Givens' failure is circuit-specific, not generic to orthogonal
attention.} Givens fails at $N{=}500$, recovers by $N{=}3{,}000$--$5{,}000$,
and fails again at $N{=}8{,}000$ (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) -- non-monotonic
in $N$, which rules out a simple memorization or under-parameterization
story. RY+CNOT, using less than a quarter of Givens' parameters and the
same orthogonality constraint, shows no such instability at any $N$. The
most parsimonious explanation is that the Givens-butterfly parameterization
induces a harder, possibly more non-convex optimization landscape than the
RY+CNOT gate sequence -- an empirical question about the two circuits'
loss landscapes that we leave to future work rather than resolve here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

RY+CNOT is a genuine, 6-qubit, NISQ-deployable gate-model quantum circuit
that produces the orthogonal attention matrices for QOVT and stays within
a point or two of a classical multi-head-attention baseline's held-out
accuracy across the data-efficiency sweep -- tied or slightly ahead up to
$N{=}5{,}000$, trailing by $0.7$--$1.4$ points at $N{=}8{,}000$ and full
data -- using $170\times$ fewer attention parameters and remaining
spectrally stable throughout; we do not claim it beats classical
attention outright. A pre-registered, held-out-test, 10-seed evaluation
shows it significantly beats an unconstrained baseline on one of two
datasets -- a real, directional, but dataset-dependent and
as-yet-unreplicated finding that a training-recipe confound (see
Limitations) means we cannot yet fully attribute to circuit choice alone.
We find no evidence that the Caro et al.\
generalization bound explains this behavior, and we show, via a classical
(non-quantum) Givens-butterfly control, that constructing "the same"
orthogonal matrix without the RY+CNOT gate sequence loses its stability
entirely, failing in roughly half of all runs including at $5\times$ more
training data.

\textbf{Limitations.} \emph{The pre-registered round's ``significant''
finding may be confounded by its training recipe, not just circuit
choice.} Classical MHA's single-seed $N{=}500$ AUC under the main
protocol (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, $0.910$) is $16$ points higher than its
10-seed mean under the pre-registered round's different, shorter recipe
(Table~\ref{tab:prereg}, $0.749$); RY+CNOT drops far less between the same
two protocols ($0.914 \to 0.886$, $2.8$ points). Sham's pre-registered
standard deviation ($0.153$, the largest of any cell in
Table~\ref{tab:prereg}) is also consistent with training instability
rather than a stable, converged comparison. We do not have per-epoch logs
from either round to confirm this directly, but the size and direction of
this asymmetry -- the larger, unconstrained arms losing far more than the
tiny RY+CNOT circuit when epochs drop from 50 to 30 and the schedule
changes -- is exactly what under-training a larger parameter space in
fewer epochs would produce. Until this is checked with a matched-recipe
control, the pre-registered Model\_II finding should be read as
\emph{RY+CNOT is more robust to this particular short training recipe than
sham or classical attention are}, which is a real result but a narrower
one than ``RY+CNOT's circuit is intrinsically better than an unconstrained
baseline.'' All results are simulated, not run on real quantum
hardware. Only the pre-registered round (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}) uses
multiple seeds; the sweep and depth ablation (Sections~\ref{sec:sweep},
\ref{sec:depth}) are single-seed and should be read as suggestive, not
conclusive. The RY+CNOT-vs-sham effect holds on Model\_II but not
Model\_III, and we do not yet know what dataset property drives that
difference. Our attention design is asymmetric ($W_Q{=}I$); we have not
tested the symmetric $W_Q{=}W_K{=}U$ variant used elsewhere in the
literature. We did not log training-accuracy trajectories in enough detail
to fully characterize \emph{why} Givens' optimization landscape is harder,
only that it is.

\textbf{Future work.} Most immediately, a matched-recipe control -- rerun
sham and classical MHA at $N{=}500$ under the \emph{main} 50-epoch
protocol with 10 seeds and the same held-out split -- to directly test
whether the recipe confound above, rather than circuit choice, explains
the Model\_II finding; an independent second pre-registered round (a
different $N$, fresh seeds) to test whether the finding replicates under
either recipe; a symmetric $W_Q{=}W_K{=}U$ variant; a loss-landscape or
Hessian-conditioning comparison between RY+CNOT and Givens to explain the
stability gap directly; a multi-seed rerun of Givens at $N{=}8{,}000$ to
characterize its failure mode more precisely; and deployment of the
RY+CNOT circuit on real NISQ hardware, for which its shallow depth and
ring connectivity make it a realistic near-term candidate.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All training code, the pre-registration document
(committed before any of these jobs ran), raw per-seed results, and this
manuscript's \LaTeX{} source are public in the project repository.
